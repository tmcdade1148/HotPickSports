# Voice References
*Cowork context file. The two reference pieces that define Tom's voice for two different jobs. Read these before drafting anything substantive.*

---

## Reference 1: The Eric Meier Reply (peer-to-peer email tuning fork)

This is Tom replying to a peer who answered a question Tom had asked weeks earlier about AI workflows. The reply is the model for: discovery outreach, follow-ups to people Tom knows, casual professional correspondence, anything written one-to-one.

What it does:
- Opens by naming the gap honestly without over-apologizing.
- Agrees with what the peer said by repeating his frame back, signaling Tom actually read it.
- Adds one small original observation. Not a thesis. An offering.
- Mentions HotPick lightly, as evidence rather than announcement.
- Undersells the punchline. *"My 25 years of storytelling experience helped too I guess."*
- Asks one specific real question.
- Closes with a low-friction call ask.
- Lowercase signature.

The piece itself:

> Yeah, that's exactly where I am. I'm super excited about it but still touching everything before it goes out the door. I love that I can work through ideas and come out the other end with something pretty cool.
>
> That side app I mentioned. It's a sports prediction platform called HotPick. Built the whole thing myself, iOS and Android, the works. Used Claude as my dev partner the whole way. Ran a full NFL season in beta last year, going public in September. Wild experience. Couldn't have built it without AI. My 25 years of storytelling experience helped too I guess.
>
> What are you feeding the models? Mostly your branded visual work, or pulling in copy and concepts too?
>
> We really should get on a call. I'd love to hear more about this.
>
> t

## Reference 2: The Thursday-Night Zoom Essay (public-writing tuning fork)

This is Tom writing publicly about his own life and work. The piece is the model for: LinkedIn longform, public essays, behind-the-scenes documentation, anything where Tom is the author and the audience is anyone.

What it does:
- Short sentences. Sometimes shorter ones underneath.
- Line breaks as a tool. Single sentences on their own lines doing real work.
- Repeats parallel structures on purpose. *"Friends I didn't choose. A street I didn't pick. A coworker I sat next to."*
- Allows contradictions to sit unresolved. *"It connects us and separates us."*
- One quiet rhetorical move per beat, never stacked.
- Undersells punchlines. *"I just showed up and paid attention."*
- Fragments where fragments work. *"Death, life, sadness, joy."*
- Closes on a small honest observation, almost a shrug. *"It took me a long time to realize that's enough."*
- No jargon, ever. The closest the piece gets to "strategy" is rejecting it.

The piece itself:

> Technology is a trip.
>
> It connects us and separates us. Makes things possible and tears other things down. Gives us everything and somehow still leaves us wanting.
>
> I've made my peace with the contradiction. Because every now and then, technology does something so unexpectedly human that you just have to give it credit.
>
> This is one of those stories.
>
> In March 2020, as the world was shutting down, a group of my closest friends decided to jump on a Zoom call on a Thursday night to catch up and figure out what was happening.
>
> We talked for almost four hours.
>
> So we scheduled another one for the following Thursday. And the Thursday after that. And the one after that.
>
> That was six years ago.
>
> We have missed maybe three Thursdays since.
>
> We talk about everything and nothing. Death, life, sadness, joy. But mostly we laugh. The kind of laughing that makes you wonder how you went so long without it.
>
> I've known these guys most of my life. Some of them since birth, a few since I was ten. I was lucky to find my people early.
>
> Technology didn't give me these friendships. It just refused to let distance end them.
>
> I didn't choose my first friends.
>
> Geography did.
>
> A handful of kids who happened to grow up on the same street. No algorithm, no common interests, no strategic networking. Just proximity and the dumb luck of where your parents decided to live.
>
> Those kids turned out to be some of the most important people in my life.
>
> I'm still talking to them every Thursday night.
>
> You spend a lot of time in life trying to find your people.
>
> Turns out mine were just outside.
>
> Early in my career I sat next to a motion designer.
>
> We weren't working on the same projects. We weren't building toward anything. We just liked each other. You know how it is. You spend enough hours next to someone, you either end up friends or you develop a very specific kind of mutual tolerance. We got lucky.
>
> She moved on. I moved on. A couple years went by.
>
> Then she called out of nowhere about a project at her new company.
>
> That call became 75 projects over the years. Seventy of them came after she'd already left that company too.
>
> I've spent a lot of time in my career trying to be in the right rooms with the right people.
>
> Turns out the right people were already there. I'm starting to think that's the whole point.
>
> A Thursday night Zoom call that's been running for six years.
>
> Friends I didn't choose. A street I didn't pick.
>
> A coworker I sat next to who became 75 projects over a decade.
>
> None of it was planned. None of it was strategic. I just showed up and paid attention.
>
> I think about this a lot because it's also how I work. The projects I'm proudest of didn't come from pitches or proposals. They came from someone picking up the phone and saying "I've got this thing and I'm not sure how to do it." And trusting me to figure it out.
>
> That's Honey + Punch. That's all it's ever been. Show up. Be real. Figure it out.
>
> I didn't build a business strategy. I just kept doing the same thing that's worked in every part of my life. Be someone people want to call when they're stuck.
>
> It took me a long time to realize that's enough.

## How to Use These References

**Before drafting an email or peer-to-peer message:** Re-read Reference 1. Match the register.

**Before drafting a LinkedIn post or public essay:** Re-read Reference 2. Match the rhythm.

**If a draft sounds wrong but you can't say why:** It's almost always one of three things. The sentences are too uniform in length. The voice reached for a forbidden word. The punchline tried to land hard instead of quiet.

**The hierarchy:** Voice fidelity matters more than completeness. A draft that's rough and right is better than a draft that's polished and wrong. If the voice is off, start over rather than edit.
